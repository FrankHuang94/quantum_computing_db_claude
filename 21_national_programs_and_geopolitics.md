# National Quantum Programs, Export Controls, and Geopolitical Dynamics

> This file covers the geopolitical dimension of quantum computing: national programs (US, China, EU, UK, and others), export controls and dual-use concerns, and — most importantly for near-term policy — the **post-quantum cryptography (PQC) transition** driven by the "harvest now, decrypt later" threat. Quantum computing's cryptography-breaking potential (Shor's algorithm, File 13) gives it a unique national-security dimension. It builds on Files 13 (Shor's algorithm), 18 (resource estimation of the cryptographic threat), 20 (the companies as national champions), and 15 (QKD vs. PQC).

## Part I — Major National Programs

### 1. US National Quantum Initiative

The **National Quantum Initiative Act (2018)** established coordinated US federal funding across NSF, DOE, and NIST (>$1.2B over five years initially, with subsequent reauthorization efforts):

- **DOE National QIS Research Centers:** five dedicated national-lab-anchored research hubs — Q-NEXT (Argonne), Co-design Center for Quantum Advantage / C²QA (Brookhaven), Superconducting Quantum Materials and Systems / SQMS (Fermilab), Quantum Science Center / QSC (Oak Ridge), and Quantum Systems Accelerator / QSA (Berkeley) — coordinating national-lab, university, and industry research.
- **NIST:** leadership in trapped-ion research (File 4), metrology (File 16), and — crucially — the **post-quantum cryptography standardization** (Part III).
- **NSF:** Quantum Leap Challenge Institutes and broad research funding.
- **DARPA:** notably the **Underexplored Systems for Utility-Scale Quantum Computing (US2QC)** program, which funds *deep technical/commercial assessments* of several hardware companies' fault-tolerant roadmaps (File 19) — a government-credibility-assessment exercise providing independent evaluation of company claims (a valuable independent-benchmarking signal, File 19).

The US program is well-funded, coordinated across agencies, anchored by national labs, and closely tied to the company landscape (File 20) via contracts and the US2QC assessment. It reflects a strategy of *coordinated national investment* leveraging the US's academic and corporate strengths.

### 2. China's quantum investment

China's quantum program is *large-scale and state-directed*:

- **Scale:** often cited at very large headline figures (a reported multi-billion-dollar national quantum lab, and large aggregate investment) — though *precise figures are not transparently disclosed*, a fact itself worth noting (the opacity is a feature).
- **USTC anchor:** the University of Science and Technology of China (Pan Jianwei's group) anchors China's program, producing high-profile **supremacy claims** — the **Jiuzhang** (photonic, File 6) and **Zuchongzhi** (superconducting, File 3) series — subject to the classical-rebuttal dynamics (File 14).
- **Communication/quantum networking:** China leads in *quantum communication* — the **Micius satellite** (File 15) and metropolitan QKD networks — a notable strength distinct from computing.
- **Commercial:** Baidu, Alibaba (historically, since scaled back), Origin Quantum — more commercially-framed but *less financially transparent* than Western public peers (File 20).

The *relative opacity* of Chinese national-program-funded efforts (compared to investor-disclosure-driven Western public companies) makes independent assessment harder (File 20) — an important caveat for roadmap-credibility assessment in this region. China's program is a major global force, especially in quantum communication (File 15), and its opacity is a recurring caveat.

### 3. EU Quantum Flagship

The **EU Quantum Flagship** is a **€1B, decade-long (2018–2028)** coordinated European research program spanning computing, communication, simulation, and sensing:

- **Scope:** one of the largest coordinated research programs, funding academic and industrial research across member states.
- **National programs within Europe:** Germany's substantial post-2021 quantum funding increase (billions of euros), France's national quantum strategy (anchoring Pasqal and Alice & Bob, File 20), the Netherlands' QuTech (a long-standing academic anchor, Files 3, 4, 7), and others.
- **Quantum Internet Alliance:** a European consortium pursuing a continental quantum-network testbed (File 15).

The EU program reflects a *coordinated continental* strategy with strong academic anchors (QuTech, Innsbruck) and emerging national champions (Pasqal, Alice & Bob, IQM). Europe's strength is its academic depth and coordinated funding, though it has fewer large commercial players than the US.

### 4. UK National Quantum Technologies Programme

The **UK National Quantum Technologies Programme (2014-onward)** is a *relatively early and sustained* national program:

- **Anchoring:** companies like Oxford Ionics, Universal Quantum, ORCA Computing (File 20), plus Rigetti UK and others.
- **Strategy:** the UK explicitly positions itself as a *hub for diverse hardware-modality startups* rather than concentrating on one approach — a "let many approaches compete" strategy.
- **Sustained funding:** one of the earliest (2014) sustained national programs, with continued investment (a National Quantum Strategy with multi-billion-pound commitments).

The UK program's early start, sustained funding, and diverse-modality strategy have made it a significant hub, punching above its size in company formation (Files 19, 20).

### 5. Other national programs

- **Australia:** Silicon Quantum Computing (UNSW-Simmons, spin qubits, File 7), Diraq, and the notable **PsiQuantum co-investment** (~$1B, File 20) — a large sovereign bet on photonics.
- **Canada:** Xanadu, D-Wave (originally), and strong academic programs (Waterloo's IQC).
- **Japan, South Korea, India, Israel, Singapore:** national quantum programs of growing scale, each cultivating academic and commercial capabilities.

The global spread of national programs reflects quantum computing's recognized *strategic importance* — nearly every major economy has a national quantum strategy, driven by the technology's economic and (especially) national-security implications (Part III).

## Part II — Export Controls and Dual-Use Concerns

### 6. Export controls

Quantum computing is subject to *export controls* reflecting its dual-use (civilian/military) and national-security dimensions:

- **US Commerce Department actions:** export controls affecting quantum-computing-related technology — cryostats (dilution refrigerators, File 11), certain control electronics, and quantum-computing *services* to certain destinations — as part of the broader technology-export-control landscape (also covered for semiconductors).
- **The dual-use tension:** quantum computing's *cryptography-breaking potential* (Shor's algorithm, File 13) gives it a *unique national-security dimension* distinct from most emerging technologies — a sufficiently capable quantum computer is a strategic asset (and threat), motivating controls on the technology and its enabling components (cryogenics, lasers, control electronics, File 11).
- **Supply-chain controls:** controls on enabling components (specialized cryogenics, ³He, File 11; lasers; control electronics) as chokepoints.

Export controls reflect quantum computing's status as a *strategic technology* — like advanced semiconductors, its most capable forms are controlled, and the enabling supply chain (cryogenics, lasers, ³He, File 11) is a control surface. This creates a geopolitical dimension to the technology's development and diffusion, affecting the competitive landscape (File 20) and international collaboration.

### 7. The cryptographic national-security dimension

Quantum computing's *unique* national-security dimension is its ability to *break public-key cryptography* via Shor's algorithm (File 13):

- A sufficiently capable quantum computer (a **cryptographically-relevant quantum computer, CRQC**) could break RSA, Diffie-Hellman, and elliptic-curve cryptography (File 13) — the foundation of essentially all current secure communication and data protection.
- This makes a CRQC a *strategic asset* (offensive: decrypting adversaries' communications) and its arrival a *strategic threat* (defensive: one's own encryption becoming vulnerable).
- The resource estimates (File 18) — millions of qubits, years away but shrinking — inform the *timeline* of this threat and drive the urgency of the defensive response (Part III).

This cryptographic dimension is what elevates quantum computing from an economic-competitiveness issue to a *national-security* one, driving both the offensive interest (building a CRQC) and the defensive response (post-quantum cryptography, Part III). It is the single most consequential geopolitical aspect of quantum computing.

## Part III — Post-Quantum Cryptography: The Dominant Near-Term Issue

### 8. The PQC transition

The **post-quantum cryptography (PQC) transition** is the *dominant near-term geopolitical/policy quantum-computing issue* — a concrete, already-in-motion response to the future quantum threat:

- **NIST's PQC standardization:** a multi-year process (2016–2024) selecting *classical* cryptographic algorithms believed hard even for quantum computers — **lattice-based** and **hash-based** algorithms. The 2024 standards: **CRYSTALS-Kyber (ML-KEM)** for key encapsulation, and **CRYSTALS-Dilithium (ML-DSA)**, **SPHINCS+ (SLH-DSA)**, and **Falcon (FN-DSA)** for digital signatures — the new classical-cryptography standards designed to resist quantum attack.
- **The nature of PQC:** PQC is *classical* cryptography (software, deployable on existing infrastructure) — *not* quantum technology. It responds to the quantum threat with quantum-resistant *classical* algorithms, distinct from QKD (File 15, hardware-based physics security). PQC is the *mainstream* response (deployable now, everywhere, in software).

The PQC transition is *already in motion* — running in *parallel* with (not waiting for) actual CRQC availability (File 18) — the concrete policy response to the quantum threat, and the largest near-term quantum-computing-related activity.

### 9. "Harvest now, decrypt later"

The specific threat model driving PQC urgency is **"harvest now, decrypt later" (HNDL)**:

- Adversaries can *record currently-encrypted traffic now* (harvesting it), and *decrypt it later* once a CRQC exists (File 18).
- This means data with *long-term sensitivity* (state secrets, personal data, intellectual property, health records) is *already at risk* — even though the CRQC doesn't yet exist — because it can be harvested today and decrypted in the future.
- Therefore, the PQC migration must happen *before* the CRQC arrives, and *for data that must remain secret for years*, the migration is *urgent now* — regardless of exactly when (or whether) a CRQC is built on the most aggressive plausible timeline (File 18).

HNDL is the key insight driving PQC urgency: the threat is *present* (data harvested now), even though the *capability* (CRQC) is future. This is why governments and organizations are migrating to PQC *now* (File 24), and why the PQC transition is the dominant near-term quantum-security issue — a rational response to a future threat with present consequences.

## Part IV — PQC Migration, Talent Policy, and Geopolitical Dynamics

### 10. The scale and challenge of PQC migration

The PQC migration (Part III) is an enormous undertaking (File 24):

- **Scope:** essentially *all* public-key cryptography — in TLS/HTTPS, VPNs, code signing, PKI, secure messaging, hardware security modules, IoT devices, and embedded systems worldwide — must migrate to PQC algorithms. This is a multi-year, global, cross-industry effort.
- **Complexity:** identifying all cryptographic dependencies (cryptographic inventory / "crypto-agility"), testing PQC algorithms (which have different performance characteristics — larger key/signature sizes), deploying updates across vast infrastructure, and handling legacy systems.
- **Hybrid deployment:** many deploy *hybrid* classical+PQC schemes (running both a classical and a PQC algorithm) during the transition, for defense-in-depth against both classical attacks (if PQC has an undiscovered weakness) and quantum attacks.
- **Government mandates:** the US (via NSM-10, CNSA 2.0, and OMB directives) and other governments have issued *timelines* mandating PQC migration for federal systems (targeting ~2030–2035 for full migration), driving the transition.

The PQC migration is arguably the *largest current dollar-value quantum-computing-adjacent economic activity* (File 24) — audits, library updates, hybrid deployments — even though it is technically a *classical-cryptography* response to the *anticipated* quantum threat, not quantum-technology spending itself. This is an important, frequently-overlooked distinction for market sizing (File 24): the biggest near-term "quantum" economic activity is *defensive classical cryptography*, not quantum technology.

### 11. Talent migration and immigration policy

An under-discussed but practically significant geopolitical factor (File 24): the concentration of quantum-research talent training in a *small number* of countries/institutions (Files 4, 5, 20) intersects with *national immigration and visa policy*:

- **Talent concentration:** the field's PhD-level talent trains at a handful of institutions (Harvard/MIT, Delft, Innsbruck, USTC, Oxford, Maryland, UCSB, File 20), and nations compete to attract and retain it.
- **Immigration policy:** a nation's ability to attract and retain foreign quantum talent (via visas, research funding, and career opportunities) is a *competitive factor* in national quantum capability — restrictive immigration can drive talent elsewhere, while welcoming policies attract it.
- **The US-China dynamic:** talent flows between the US and China (many top researchers trained in the US, some returning to China) are a sensitive geopolitical issue, intersecting with export controls (Part II) and security concerns.

Talent migration and immigration policy are thus a *quiet but significant* geopolitical dimension — a nation's quantum competitiveness depends partly on attracting and retaining the scarce PhD-level talent (File 24), making immigration policy a lever of national quantum strategy. This intersects with the broader talent-concentration dynamics (File 20) and the AI/ML talent competition (File 20).

### 12. The geopolitical competition framing

Quantum computing is often framed as a *geopolitical competition* — a "quantum race" — particularly between the US and China:

- **The competition narrative:** whichever nation/bloc achieves a CRQC first gains a strategic cryptographic advantage (Part II), and quantum computing (like AI and semiconductors) is seen as a *strategic technology* where leadership matters for economic and national security.
- **The reality check:** the competition is real but the *technology* is early (a handful of logical qubits, File 9) and the *timeline* long (File 18) — so the "race" is a long-horizon competition for *capability*, not an imminent finish line. Supremacy claims (File 14) are contested and not equivalent to a CRQC.
- **The collaboration reality:** despite the competition framing, quantum science is substantially *international and collaborative* (open publication, international talent, cross-border partnerships) — the competition coexists with collaboration, complicating a pure "race" narrative.

The geopolitical-competition framing is real (nations invest for strategic reasons, export controls reflect it) but should be *calibrated*: it is a long-horizon competition for capability (File 18), coexisting with international scientific collaboration, not an imminent zero-sum race. Overheating the "quantum race" narrative (like the AI race) can distort policy and investment (File 24), while ignoring the genuine strategic dimension (Part II) underestimates it — the disciplined view holds both.

### 13. Worked analysis: assessing the CRQC timeline for policy

Policymakers must assess *when* a CRQC (File 18) might exist to plan the PQC migration (Part III). Applying resource estimation (File 18):

- **Current hardware:** a handful of logical qubits (File 9); a CRQC needs thousands of logical qubits = millions of physical qubits (File 18).
- **The gap:** ~1000× logical qubits, a large but shrinking gap (File 18).
- **The timeline:** resource estimation (File 18) plus roadmaps (File 19) suggest a CRQC is plausibly a *2030s-and-beyond* prospect, with substantial uncertainty (File 18).
- **The policy response:** because of HNDL (Section 9) and the long migration time (Section 10), the PQC migration must happen *now* — the *uncertainty* in the CRQC timeline, combined with the *present* HNDL threat and the *multi-year* migration, justifies *immediate* migration regardless of the exact CRQC date.

This worked analysis shows how resource estimation (File 18) informs policy (File 21): the CRQC timeline is uncertain and long, but the HNDL threat and migration time justify *acting now* — a rational response to a future threat under uncertainty. It exemplifies the resource-estimation-to-policy connection (File 18), and it is why the PQC migration (Part III) is *already in motion* despite the CRQC being years away.

## Part V — Strategic Assessment, Glossary, and Summary

### 14. Comparing national strategies

The major programs reflect distinct *national strategies*:

| Program | Strategy | Strength | Distinctive feature |
|---|---|---|---|
| US (NQI) | Coordinated investment + industry | Academic + corporate depth, capital | DARPA US2QC independent assessment |
| China | State-directed, large-scale | Quantum communication (Micius) | Opacity; supremacy-claim cadence |
| EU Flagship | Coordinated continental research | Academic depth (QuTech, Innsbruck) | €1B decade program |
| UK NQTP | Diverse-modality hub | Early start, startup formation | "Let many approaches compete" |
| Australia | Focused sovereign bets | Spin qubits (SQC), PsiQuantum | Large sovereign co-investment |

The strategies vary — US (industry + coordinated investment + independent assessment), China (state-directed, communication-strong, opaque), EU (coordinated continental research), UK (diverse startup hub), Australia (focused sovereign bets) — reflecting different national strengths and approaches. No single strategy is clearly best; they reflect each nation's academic base, industrial structure, and strategic priorities. The competition among these strategies (Section 12) is a long-horizon test of which national approach best cultivates quantum capability.

### 15. The dual response: offense and defense

Nations pursue a *dual response* to quantum computing's cryptographic dimension (Part II):

- **Offense (building a CRQC):** national programs (Part I) fund quantum-computing development, partly motivated by the strategic value of a CRQC (Part II) — the offensive interest in eventually breaking adversaries' cryptography.
- **Defense (PQC migration):** simultaneously, nations migrate to PQC (Part III) to protect their *own* cryptography against a future CRQC (their own or an adversary's).

This dual response — building the capability while defending against it — is characteristic of strategic dual-use technologies, and it means nations invest in *both* quantum computing (offense/capability) *and* PQC migration (defense). The PQC migration (Part III, defensive) is the *near-term* priority (deployable now, urgent due to HNDL), while quantum-computing development (offensive/capability) is the *long-term* investment (File 18). Both are driven by the same underlying cryptographic dimension (Part II).

### 16. QKD vs. PQC in national strategy (recap from File 15)

Nations differ in their emphasis on QKD (File 15) vs. PQC (Part III) for the defensive response:

- **PQC (mainstream):** the US and most Western nations prioritize *PQC* (classical, software-deployable, addresses the full stack) as the primary defense — NIST's standardization (Part III) leads globally.
- **QKD (China's emphasis):** China has invested heavily in *QKD* (File 15) — the Micius satellite and metro QKD networks — as a physics-based defensive layer, reflecting a different strategic emphasis (though QKD is niche and hardware-heavy, File 15, and Western agencies like NSA have expressed skepticism about QKD's practicality vs. PQC).

The QKD-vs-PQC divergence in national strategy (File 15) reflects different assessments of the two approaches — PQC (mainstream, practical) vs. QKD (physics-based, niche) — with China notably more invested in QKD. This is a real strategic divergence, and the PQC-vs-QKD distinction (File 15) is important for understanding national quantum-security strategies.

### 17. Glossary

- **National Quantum Initiative (NQI):** the 2018 US federal quantum program (NSF, DOE, NIST).
- **DOE QIS Research Centers:** five national-lab-anchored US research hubs (Q-NEXT, C²QA, SQMS, QSC, QSA).
- **DARPA US2QC:** the program funding independent assessments of companies' fault-tolerant roadmaps.
- **EU Quantum Flagship:** the €1B, decade-long EU research program.
- **CRQC (cryptographically-relevant quantum computer):** a quantum computer capable of breaking public-key cryptography (Shor, File 13).
- **Post-quantum cryptography (PQC):** classical algorithms resistant to quantum attack (NIST-standardized: ML-KEM/Kyber, ML-DSA/Dilithium, SLH-DSA/SPHINCS+).
- **Harvest now, decrypt later (HNDL):** recording encrypted data now to decrypt once a CRQC exists — drives PQC urgency.
- **Crypto-agility:** the ability to update cryptographic algorithms readily — a goal of the PQC migration.
- **Export controls:** restrictions on quantum-technology and enabling-component export for national security.
- **Micius:** China's quantum-communication satellite (File 15).

### 18. Reader's takeaway and summary

The geopolitical dimension of quantum computing centers on its *unique national-security significance* — the ability to break public-key cryptography (Shor's algorithm, File 13) — which drives both national investment (offense/capability) and the post-quantum-cryptography migration (defense). Major national programs (US NQI with its coordinated investment and DARPA US2QC assessment; China's large, opaque, communication-strong program; the EU Quantum Flagship; the UK's diverse-modality hub; and sovereign bets like Australia's PsiQuantum co-investment) reflect quantum computing's recognized strategic importance, and export controls treat it (like advanced semiconductors) as a controlled strategic technology with a controllable enabling supply chain (cryogenics, ³He, lasers, File 11). The *dominant near-term issue* is the **PQC transition** — NIST's standardized classical algorithms (Kyber/ML-KEM, Dilithium/ML-DSA) deployed *now*, driven by the **"harvest now, decrypt later"** threat that makes long-term-sensitive data *already at risk* even though the CRQC (File 18) is years away — arguably the largest current quantum-computing-adjacent economic activity (though technically a classical-cryptography response, File 24). The geopolitical-competition framing (the "quantum race," especially US-China) is real but should be calibrated: a long-horizon competition for capability (File 18) coexisting with international scientific collaboration, not an imminent zero-sum finish. Talent migration and immigration policy are a quiet but significant competitive factor (File 24), and the QKD-vs-PQC divergence (File 15, China's QKD emphasis vs. the West's PQC mainstream) reflects differing defensive strategies. The disciplined view holds both the genuine strategic dimension (cryptography, national security) and the calibrated timeline (CRQC years away, File 18), driving the rational near-term response — PQC migration now — while the long-term capability competition unfolds. The geopolitics of quantum computing is thus dominated by the cryptographic dimension: build the capability (offense, long-term), defend against it (PQC, near-term), and compete/collaborate internationally (strategic, calibrated) — the policy face of the technology whose physics, resources, and companies the database's other files develop.

## Appendix — PQC Migration Practicalities and Policy Timeline

### The NIST PQC standards (2024)

- **ML-KEM (FIPS 203, formerly CRYSTALS-Kyber):** lattice-based key encapsulation — the primary key-exchange standard.
- **ML-DSA (FIPS 204, formerly CRYSTALS-Dilithium):** lattice-based digital signatures — the primary signature standard.
- **SLH-DSA (FIPS 205, formerly SPHINCS+):** hash-based (stateless) signatures — a conservative backup relying only on hash-function security.
- **FN-DSA (Falcon):** lattice-based signatures for size-constrained applications (standardization ongoing).

The reliance on *lattice-based* cryptography (for the primary standards) rests on the conjectured hardness of lattice problems even for quantum computers — including the non-abelian hidden-subgroup problem's difficulty (File 13), which is *not* known to be efficiently quantum-solvable. The *hash-based* backup (SLH-DSA) provides diversity in case lattice cryptography has an undiscovered weakness (classical or quantum) — a hedge reflecting cryptographic conservatism.

### The migration timeline (US federal, representative)

- **2022:** NSM-10 directs federal agencies to prepare for PQC migration; OMB requires cryptographic inventories.
- **2024:** NIST finalizes the first PQC standards (FIPS 203/204/205).
- **~2025–2030:** federal agencies migrate priority systems; CNSA 2.0 timelines mandate PQC for national-security systems.
- **~2030–2035:** target for broad federal migration completion.

Other governments (EU, UK, and others) have parallel timelines. The multi-year horizon reflects the migration's scale (Section 10) — and the HNDL threat (Section 9) means the migration must proceed *now* for long-term-sensitive data, even as it completes over the coming decade.

### Practical migration challenges

- **Cryptographic inventory:** organizations must first *find* all their cryptographic dependencies (often poorly documented) — a prerequisite and a major effort.
- **Performance:** PQC algorithms have *larger* keys/signatures and different performance profiles, requiring testing and sometimes protocol/infrastructure changes.
- **Legacy systems:** embedded and legacy systems with long lifecycles (industrial, IoT, satellites) are hard to update, creating long-lived vulnerabilities.
- **Hybrid deployment:** running classical+PQC in parallel during the transition adds complexity but provides defense-in-depth.
- **Crypto-agility:** building systems that can *readily* update algorithms (in case a PQC standard is later broken) is a long-term goal.

These challenges make the PQC migration a substantial, multi-year, cross-industry undertaking (File 24) — the concrete, near-term face of quantum computing's policy impact, driven by a *future* threat (the CRQC, File 18) with *present* consequences (HNDL, Section 9). It is the clearest example of quantum computing shaping policy and economic activity *today*, years before a useful quantum computer exists — a striking illustration of how the *anticipated* capability (File 18) drives concrete present action, and the dominant near-term geopolitical/policy dimension of the technology.

### Final note

The geopolitics of quantum computing is dominated by its cryptographic dimension: nations invest in the capability (national programs, Part I), control its diffusion (export controls, Part II), and — most concretely and urgently — migrate to post-quantum cryptography (Part III) against the harvest-now-decrypt-later threat, all while competing and collaborating internationally on a long-horizon timeline (File 18). The PQC migration is the near-term policy priority and the largest quantum-adjacent economic activity, driven by resource-estimation-informed (File 18) assessment of a future threat with present consequences. Understanding this geopolitical dimension — the strategic significance, the dual offense/defense response, the calibrated competition, and the concrete PQC migration — completes the policy face of quantum computing, complementing the technology (Files 2–13), resources (File 18), and commercial landscape (File 20) the database develops, and grounding the technology's societal and strategic implications in the disciplined, evidence-based assessment the database maintains throughout.
